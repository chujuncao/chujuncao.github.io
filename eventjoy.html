EVENT & SOCIAL APP — STUDENT PROJECT (INF1611)

EventJoy

You just landed in a new city. There's an event tonight that looks interesting — but is it actually safe to go to, alone?

Team

Full team project, individual final mockup

My focus

Competitive research, UI planning, testing, final UI

Process

Research → Prototype → Testing → Iteration

PROBLEM

International students who just arrived in Toronto struggle to find events they can trust. Information is scattered across social media, university pages, and group chats, making it hard to judge credibility, know what an event will actually be like, or feel confident attending alone.

SOLUTION

EventJoy curates local events with clear organizer information, honest event details, and visible social reassurance — so a newcomer can judge whether an event is trustworthy and right for them before deciding to go.

MY ROLE

This was a full team project spanning six stages: background research, primary research, UI planning, prototyping, usability testing, and a final report. I authored the competitive research individually, contributed to interviews, UI planning, and testing, designed the Homepage, Recommendation, and Organizer Info screens, and built the final high-fidelity mockup on my own.

Where I started: competitive research

Before any interviews, I analyzed three existing platforms international students might already turn to — to see where the real gap was.

Eventbrite

An efficient, transactional marketplace — but the grid of competing event cards is overwhelming for a newcomer, and it offers no social trust signals.

Meetup

Community-centered, with attendee counts that build social proof — but discovery relies on already knowing what to search for, which a newcomer lacks.

Eventful

Built entirely for event organizers, not attendees — functionally unusable for someone trying to discover something to go to.

The gap: no existing product combined curated discoverability with real trust signals and community reassurance — which became EventJoy's core bet.

Who we designed for

Primary persona · Year 2, Arts & Science, age 20

From China, two years into living in Toronto, first language Mandarin, on a student budget. Regularly attends cultural events — film screenings, art shows, live music — and follows university clubs and Toronto event pages on social media to keep up with what's happening.

She wants an easy way to find out about events in time, ideally with a single, simple booking flow — and to go with friends rather than alone. In her own words, the hardest part is often just finding out an event exists before it sells out, and current apps rarely let her share plans through the tools she already uses with friends.

What 8 interviews told us

As a team, we interviewed 8 international students about how they currently find events — I personally conducted 2 of these interviews.

  • Trust is scattered, not absent. Event information exists — across social media, university pages, group chats — but nothing helps students judge what's actually credible.
  • Suitability is hard to judge from a listing. Vibe, location, price, and crowd type are all unclear before students arrive.
  • Attending alone is the real barrier. Many students want a way to sense who else might be there before committing to go by themselves.

From insight to structure

The team translated these findings into personas, a journey map, a storyboard, and task flows — then wireframes covering the homepage, recommendations, event details, organizer info, and booking. I personally created the persona, the to-be journey map, and the storyboard used to align the team on the user's goals and pain points, and later designed the Homepage, Recommendation, and Organizer Info screens in the prototype.

Testing it, then fixing it

The team ran usability testing with 8 international students — I personally conducted 2 of these sessions. Three issues stood out clearly enough to act on.

ISSUE 1 — Organizer info felt static

Participants weren't sure the organizer card could be tapped — it looked like a rating display, not a link.

Change: Removed the rating/stars and replaced them with a clearly styled "View Organizer →" text link.

ISSUE 2 — Prices were hidden until you opened an event

Participants wanted to compare costs across events without tapping into each one individually.

Change: Added a visible price tag directly on every event card in the list.

ISSUE 3 — Share icons had no labels

Several icons on the share screen weren't self-explanatory, causing hesitation about what each one would do.

Change: Removed unrelated icons and added short text labels under the ones that remained.

Finding the right tone

Before landing on a final look, I translated the team's three communication goals — belonging, trust, and clarity — into three separate style tiles, each built around one goal, then chose the direction that felt truest to what the app needed to say.

Warm & Community — chosen

Built around the belonging goal — coral tones and candid, golden-hour photography that felt inviting rather than transactional, closer to how showing up to an event with new friends should feel.

Trustworthy & Neutral

Built around the trust goal — cool blues and imagery like handshakes and city streets that read as credible and safe, but felt closer to a corporate booking tool than a social app for making friends.

Minimal & Straightforward

Built around the clarity goal — black and white with no visual noise, fast to scan but flat and impersonal for a product meant to feel welcoming.

Why Warm & Community won: the warmer palette matched the feeling the app was actually meant to create — the other two directions read as safer or cleaner, but colder.

Outcome

The final prototype carried clear navigation, visible prices, a clickable organizer link, and labeled share actions — small changes on paper, but ones that directly addressed the confidence gap newcomers described in interviews. It confirmed a pattern I'd keep relying on: test early, because assumptions are often wrong, and clarity does more for usability than decoration.
