COOKING & SOCIAL APP — STUDENT PROJECT (INF2191)

FlyingSaucers

Ashley cooks with her sister every Sunday. Her sister lives across the country.

Team

Group research & concept, individual continuation

My focus

Usability testing, iteration, visual direction

Timeline

2 course sprints

PROBLEM

People who move away from family — across cities, provinces, or countries — lose the small rituals that used to hold them together. Video calls during meals feel forced and tiring to schedule. Recipe apps are built for cooking alone, not for cooking with someone who isn't in the room.

SOLUTION

FlyingSaucers lets two people follow the same recipe on their own schedule — same week, different times — then share photos and reactions afterward. No one has to be online at the same time for it to feel shared.

MY ROLE

The research and concept (personas, task flows, testing plan) were built with my group. I then continued the project individually: running usability testing, synthesizing findings into concrete design changes, and finalizing the visual direction.

Who it's for

Ashley · 22, primary user

International student living alone in Toronto. Cooks 3–4 times a week, on an irregular school schedule. Wants a low-pressure way to stay close to family.

Camila · 27, secondary user

Ashley's sister, working full-time in Vancouver. Busy and often cooks after work. Finds scheduled video calls feel like another obligation.

What research told us

  • Connection without a schedule. People don't want another video call — they want to feel close without needing to be online at the same time.
  • The ritual matters more than the tech. Recreating one specific family recipe carried far more emotional weight than "cooking together" in general.
  • Asynchronous still counts as shared. Comparing photos and results after the fact was enough to make people feel like they'd done it together.
  • Distance creates real friction. Some ingredients aren't available in every city, so recipes needed to account for substitutions.
  • Clarity affects trust in the ritual. During testing, unclear icons and labels caused hesitation at exactly the moments meant to feel warm and easy.

Testing it, then fixing it

I ran moderated usability sessions with 2 participants over video call, plus a peer design critique, then translated what surfaced into three concrete changes.

ISSUE 1 — An icon meant two different things

The same "+" icon was used to save a recipe on one screen and to select a recipe on another. Participants weren't sure which action it meant, and hesitated before tapping it.

Change: Split into two distinct controls — a bookmark icon for saving, and a labeled "Select Recipe" button for the activity flow.

ISSUE 2 — Filters applied one at a time

Selecting a single filter (e.g. difficulty) instantly updated the results, so participants couldn't combine filters the way they expected to.

Change: Redesigned filtering to allow multiple selections before applying, with a clear "Apply" button to confirm.

ISSUE 3 — Navigation didn't match expectations

The "Add" tab didn't say what it was for, and recipe cards looked clickable but weren't — participants tapped them expecting a detail page.

Change: Renamed "Add" to "Activity," and made recipe cards navigate to a full detail page on tap.

Finding the right tone

I explored three visual directions before choosing one to refine.

Warm & Social — chosen

Soft, warm tones that felt inviting and comfortable for a social cooking ritual — refined further to reduce visual competition between colors.

Clean & Minimal

Clear and organized, but read as neutral — it lacked the warmth the social, family-oriented concept needed.

Playful & Friendly

Energetic and eye-catching at first glance, but multiple strong colors made it harder to keep a consistent visual hierarchy.

Outcome

Both participants completed both test tasks — finding a recipe and inviting a friend to cook it together. The friction that did surface was consistently about interface clarity (icons and labels), which the iteration above addressed directly. Participants responded well to the overall layout, especially how clearly cooking time and difficulty were presented, which helped them decide quickly.

What I did, specifically

  • Planned and moderated 4 remote usability testing sessions, using a think-aloud protocol
  • Synthesized session notes and peer critique into 3 prioritized, actionable issues
  • Redesigned the affected interactions: save/select icons, filter behavior, and navigation labeling
  • Explored and evaluated 3 visual directions, then selected and refined the final style
