INFORMATION ARCHITECTURE — INDIVIDUAL PROJECT (INF2170)

The Well-Faceted Coffee Shop

Your friends built a coffee storefront with every possible filter. It's a wonderland. It's also unusable.

Role

Individual — research, IA, and testing

Method

Heuristic evaluation, discount usability test

Deliverables

Facet decisions, 2 IA models, test results

PROBLEM

A coffee storefront tried to filter by every attribute it could measure — cultivar, processing, origin, flavor notes, acidity, two brewing-method fields, even altitude buckets. The result was technically complete and practically unusable: a facet panel nobody could scan.

SOLUTION

I evaluated every facet against real-world data conditions — not the tidy class dataset — then designed two different, equally valid ways to browse the same catalog: one organized by region, one by flavor.

MY ROLE

Solo project. The competitive scan, both IA structures, and a discount usability test with 3 participants are all my own work, start to finish.

What real coffee shops actually facet

I scanned four specialty-coffee retailers (Intelligentsia, Blue Bottle, Onyx Coffee Lab, Stumptown) and scored every candidate facet against four heuristics — Popularity, Coverage, Utility, and UI Fit — using real-world data conditions rather than the class dataset's artificially complete records.

  • Real shops keep it small. Most rely on a short, edited list — Region, Roast Level, Processing Method, sometimes a high-level Flavor Category. Richer fields like Cultivar or Acidity live in product descriptions, but are almost never used as filters.
  • Coverage in theory ≠ coverage in practice. Fields like Origin, Roast, and Processing Method are standardized and reliably filled in. Flavor Profile, Cultivar, and Harvest Year are frequently missing or inconsistent — and would degrade the experience as the catalog grows.
  • Price is everywhere, but never a facet. Every site let you sort by price, but none faceted it. I kept a simple price-range facet anyway — it supports a basic purchasing need without adding UI clutter.

Three recommendations came out of this scan: merge scattered flavor-note fields into standardized flavor families, standardize brewing-method labels into one controlled vocabulary, and make Region a navigation anchor while keeping Origin on product pages to avoid redundancy.

Testing the flavor-first model

A 5-minute discount usability test with 3 participants of varying coffee-buying experience, each completing two findability tasks on the flavor-first IA.

Selected Fruity immediately and explicitly ignored "Tiny Flavor Notes," calling it too narrow for a first pass. Fastest of the three.

  • The category/sub-category hierarchy wasn't clear. Two of three participants treated flavor notes and flavor categories as interchangeable, and one had to manually resolve a duplicate selection across levels.
  • Expertise changes what "clear" means. The frequent buyer moved fastest by deliberately ignoring the detail layer — flavor-first favored people who already knew what they wanted more than first-time browsers.

What I'd test next

A comparative tree test between the flavor-first and region-first structures would show which model actually performs better for which kind of shopper — a beginner versus an experienced buyer — rather than assuming one structure fits everyone.

What I did, specifically

  • Scanned 4 competitor sites and scored every facet against Popularity, Coverage, Utility, and UI Fit
  • Designed a minimal, mobile-friendly primary IA organized by region, roast, and processing
  • Designed an alternative flavor-first IA, renaming and restructuring facets around taste
  • Planned and ran a discount usability test with 3 participants across 2 findability tasks
  • Synthesized test results into a specific hierarchy issue and a recommendation for further testing
